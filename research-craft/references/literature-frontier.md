# Literature and Frontier Research

Read for literature review, state-of-the-art comparison, novelty assessment, or rapidly changing technical knowledge.

## Scope first

Turn the request into a focused question with concepts, synonyms, date range, disciplines, source types, and inclusion/exclusion criteria. For a formal review, record the search date, queries, databases or indexes actually accessible, screening logic, and coverage limitations. Do not imply a systematic review without a systematic protocol.

Name the review type honestly:

- `rapid`: time-bounded, targeted evidence scan with explicit coverage limits;
- `scoping`: maps concepts, methods, and gaps without claiming exhaustive effect synthesis;
- `systematic`: protocol-driven search, deduplication, screening, extraction, and study-quality assessment;
- `narrative`: expert synthesis without systematic coverage claims;
- `single-paper review`: deep evaluation of one work, not a literature review.

Pilot the search before scaling it. Confirm that known sentinel papers are retrievable, inspect false positives and false negatives, refine synonyms and controlled vocabulary, and then freeze the formal query. Search multiple relevant indexes when the review type requires broad coverage; database count is not a substitute for domain coverage.

For a systematic review, define the question framework appropriate to the domain, sources, complete reproducible queries, search dates, inclusion/exclusion criteria, deduplication rule, title/abstract and full-text screening decisions, extraction fields, and risk-of-bias or study-quality method before synthesis. Record each search with the `writing search` registry and each included or consequential work in `literature.csv`. If these elements are absent, downgrade the label rather than borrowing the authority of “systematic.”

## Source hierarchy

Prefer original papers, standards, official documentation, datasets, protocols, and author repositories. Use reviews to map a field and discover primary sources, not as automatic proof of each claim. Verify retractions, corrections, publication status, version, and whether a preprint differs from the final paper when consequential.

Read beyond titles and snippets. Check methods, population/data, baseline fairness, metrics, uncertainty, limitations, supplementary material, and whether the cited result has the same scope as the proposed claim. Seek contradictory and null evidence, not only confirming work.

Screen in explicit stages (`title/abstract -> full text`) with inclusion/exclusion reasons and deduplication rules. For high-stakes or systematic work, use independent dual screening or document why it was not feasible. Extract into a common schema before synthesis so differences in population, intervention/exposure, comparator, outcome, design, setting, and risk of bias are visible.

## Frontier and novelty

Current or "latest" claims require a fresh search and explicit as-of date. Separate first publication, independent replication, best reported result, practical adoption, and consensus. A novelty claim is bounded by the sources and indexes searched; phrase it as "we did not identify" rather than "no prior work exists" unless exhaustive evidence supports that statement.

Build a compact synthesis around agreements, disagreements, methodological differences, evidence quality, open questions, and decision relevance. Never fabricate bibliographic metadata, quotations, DOIs, or access to a paper. Preserve `UNKNOWN` when the full source could not be inspected.

Synthesize by question or theme rather than writing one paragraph per paper. Separate direct evidence, indirect evidence, methodological explanation for disagreement, and inference. Citation count, venue prestige, and source volume do not replace applicability, independence, or study quality.

Organize conclusions as `well supported`, `contested`, and `unknown`. Count papers only as a description of coverage; evidence strength depends on relevance, methods, independence, and quality, not source volume. Stop searching when added sources no longer change a decision-relevant conclusion or expose a material gap, while stating the databases and dates that bound that judgment.
